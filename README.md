
# Development Story

SCOUT originally started as a physical reconnaissance rover project.

My original idea was to build the whole thing as a working rover, with tracks, motors, sonar and software all working together. I worked on this during the Maker Challenge, where I designed and built the prototype and experimented with a lot of equipment and processes that were new to me.

As I got further into the project, though, I realised that I wasn't going to have enough time to make every part of the rover fully functional without rushing everything.

So instead, I split the project into a few main parts:

- 🛰️ Sonar / radar
- 🛞 Tracks
- 💻 Software

The sonar and software ended up being the parts I was able to get working best.

I built the ultrasonic system using an HC-SR04 and an SG90 servo, then connected it to a Raspberry Pi Pico W. I also made a web interface that could display the sonar readings. This was one of the parts I was most happy with because it was my first time working with a lot of the equipment and processes involved.

The tracks didn't go quite as planned. Some of the parts that had been printed were designed around a different motor than the one we ended up needing. Because of the time limit, I couldn't properly get the motors and tracks working together. I still made a makeshift version of the track system so I could experiment with the mechanical side of SCOUT.

So by the end of the Maker Challenge, I didn't have a completely working rover. I did, however, have a working sonar/radar system and software, as well as a physical prototype with the mechanical parts designed and built.

## Taking SCOUT Further

After finishing the Maker Challenge, I didn't really want to just leave SCOUT there.

I decided to take the idea and do something completely separate with it: **make a web-based interactive demo/game based around SCOUT.**

Instead of someone just looking at photos of the physical rover, I wanted them to actually be able to interact with the idea behind it.

That's what this project is.

The web demo lets people experience SCOUT digitally, and it has also given me a chance to keep developing the software side of the project. I've been experimenting with things like movement, interactions, dialogue, missions, mobile controls and the overall game experience.

I think this is a pretty cool way of continuing the project because the physical SCOUT prototype showed me what I could build with hardware, while the web demo lets me take the same idea in a completely different direction.

## Why I Made the Demo

One of the biggest things I learnt from making the physical SCOUT was that projects don't always go exactly how you planned them.

I originally wanted everything to work, but when I realised that wasn't realistic within the time I had, I had to change what I was trying to achieve.

Instead of seeing that as the end of the project, I decided to keep the idea going and build something new around it.

The web demo is basically my way of doing that.

It's not supposed to pretend that the original rover was completely finished. It's a new project that grew out of the original SCOUT idea and lets me explore the concept from a software/game-development perspective.

## AI Usage

I used AI as a development tool while working on the web demo.

I mainly used it when I was stuck on programming problems, trying to understand unfamiliar JavaScript concepts, debugging code, or thinking through possible ways to implement something.

I didn't use AI to come up with the whole project or replace my own work. I decided what I wanted the demo to be, designed how it should work, tested it, changed things when they didn't work, and put the different parts together myself.

AI was basically another tool I could use when I got stuck, alongside documentation, testing and figuring things out myself.

## What I Learnt

The original SCOUT project taught me a lot about hardware and engineering, especially because so much of the equipment and manufacturing process was new to me.

The web demo has been teaching me a different side of the same project: software development and turning an engineering idea into something that other people can actually interact with.

Most importantly, I've learnt that a project doesn't have to go exactly according to the original plan.

Sometimes you have to change the scope, work around something that failed, or take the idea in a completely different direction.

SCOUT started as a physical rover.

Now it's also a game.

And I think that's a much more interesting place for the project to go next.
